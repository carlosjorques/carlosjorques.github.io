I have spent my career inside engine and vehicle control units: first developing combustion control software for diesel engines, later control software for some of the first electrified heavy trucks at Scania, and along the way a PhD at Lund University on in-cycle closed-loop combustion control ([Jorques Moreno, 2021](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/publication/afeb6cfc-2aa4-4230-b51e-492b9376791e)). That vantage point, the millisecond loop where software meets physics, is a good place to watch an industry change. And the industry has changed more in the last eight years than in the previous forty.

This article is my attempt to connect the dots: how European automotive companies organized software development, why that organization stopped working, what the popular escape routes actually deliver, and what I think a competitive organization looks like now. It is also the entry point of a series. Three companion articles go deeper into the individual arguments: [The Real Cost of Automotive Software Is Learning Speed](/writing/real-cost-of-automotive-software-speed/) on what engineering speed actually is, [The Externalization Trap in Automotive Software](/writing/externalization-trap-software-defined-world/) on how sourcing decisions destroy it, and [AI Will Not Replace Automotive Engineers](/writing/ai-will-not-replace-automotive-engineers/) on what AI does to an engineering organization, for better and worse. Together, the four discuss how the automotive industry is transforming around its software development processes in the era of the software-defined vehicle.

## Two ways to build vehicle software

European OEMs historically made a clear choice: they were not software companies, so they wouldn't act like ones. Software-heavy features were specified in exhaustive requirement documents and handed to suppliers. Frameworks like Automotive SPICE and the V-model grew around this paradigm and became genuinely good at what they were designed for: traceability, quality assurance, and coordination across a supply chain of dozens of companies.

But listen to how Ford's CEO Jim Farley described the end state of that model in 2023: *"We farmed out all the modules that control the vehicles to our suppliers because we could bid them against each other... we have about 150 modules with software written by 150 different companies. They don't talk to each other. So even though it says Ford on the front, I actually have to go to Bosch to get permission to change their seat-control software"* ([The Drive](https://www.thedrive.com/news/ford-ceo-explains-why-legacy-automakers-take-forever-to-issue-ota-updates)). The IP sits with the supplier, the languages differ, and a simple over-the-air update becomes a diplomatic negotiation. Farley has since called software a challenge *"ten times bigger"* than EVs or China ([InsideEVs, 2026](https://insideevs.com/news/788731/jim-farley-software-transition/)).

There was always a second model, and I was lucky to work inside it. Scania, like a few other engineering-led companies, kept control software development in-house, and kept the skills in-house with it. The difference was visceral. When a mechanical engineer needed a new function or hit a problem, they walked over to our desk. We could have something running on a test truck in days or weeks, depending on complexity, not after a requirements round-trip through a supplier's change-management board. Sweden's flat hierarchies helped; nobody needed permission from three management layers to talk to us.

The deeper advantage was understanding. We sat close enough to the hardware to know the machine, not just the interface spec. We drove the trucks we programmed. That matters more than it sounds: when you're the one driving, you know that the AC compressor just kicked in, that the truck ahead is braking, that the hill starts *here*. So when you analyze the logs afterward, you're confirming expectations rather than reconstructing reality from signal traces. A test engineer executing someone else's test specification at a test track produces cleaner, more repeatable data. But the developer reading that data afterward misses the unanticipated behavior, can't adjust the experiment mid-run, and loses everything the sensors didn't capture. Each translation step between the person who understands the function and the person who observes the system leaks understanding. [The learning-speed article](/writing/real-cost-of-automotive-software-speed/) builds this argument in full: the loop from observed physical behavior to validated software change is the real engineering KPI.

To be fair: neither model is simply better. They optimize for different things. The externalized model optimizes for *process*: it scales across a supply chain and produces auditable quality. The in-house model optimizes for *cross-competence*: it produces short learning loops and engineers who understand the whole system. For decades, both worked well enough that the choice looked like a matter of corporate taste.

Then the market changed.

## The shock: when the heart of the car became software

Two events broke the old equilibrium. Tesla proved that a serious car could be built around a centralized computing architecture, with features delivered and improved over the air. And Dieselgate, which has cost Volkswagen more than €31 billion in fines and settlements ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/business/volkswagen-says-diesel-scandal-has-cost-it-313-billion-euros-idUSKBN2141JA/)), exposed how much strain stringent emissions legislation had put on the old way of working. Meeting Euro 6 was never a purely mechanical problem; it demanded more sensors, more flexible actuation, after-treatment systems, and far more sophisticated control software. The engineering center of gravity had quietly moved from the engine to the code, and the organizations hadn't moved with it.

Meanwhile the legacy architecture problem compounded. Every new feature historically meant another ECU, more wiring, more CAN buses already at their bandwidth limits. When Ford tore down a Tesla, Farley admitted the Mach-E's wiring harness was 1.6 kilometers longer and 70 pounds heavier than Tesla's ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/788731/jim-farley-software-transition/)). This is what the software-defined vehicle transition really is: collapsing 150 supplier-owned black boxes into a centralized, OEM-owned software platform, while simultaneously keeping legacy platforms alive and profitable. Volkswagen founded Cariad to do exactly this in-house; it accumulated over $7.5 billion in operating losses between 2022 and 2024 and its delays pushed back the Porsche Macan EV and Audi Q6 e-tron by years ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/753673/vw-group-cariad-billions-losses-2024/)). McKinsey saw this coming: automotive software complexity is growing far faster than development productivity, with code bases jumping from ~10 million lines in 2010 to ~150 million by 2016 ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/automotive-and-assembly/our-insights/rethinking-car-software-and-electronics-architecture)).

## The affordability spiral

All of this transformation costs money, and the money shows up on the sticker. In Germany, an entry-level combustion small car that cost €13,041 in 2014 costs €22,591 today, a 73% increase, while a third of small-car models simply disappeared from the market ([ADAC via Vision Mobility](https://vision-mobility.de/en/news/adac-small-cars-becoming-fewer-and-more-expensive-e-cars-averaging-30-000-euros-326134.html)). JATO Dynamics calculates that the average retail price of a car in Germany now exceeds the average German pre-tax annual salary, and that prices in Italy and the UK are up 45% since 2019 ([JATO](https://www.jato.com/hubfs/Whitepapers/Rising%20car%20prices%20and%20their%20impact%20on%20Europes%20automotive%20industry%20-%20JATO.pdf)). The joint JATO and Oliver Wyman pricing study puts numbers on the squeeze: German new-car prices rose 40% between 2019 and 2024 while volumes fell 22%, only about a third of the increase is attributable to electrification, and despite net salaries rising 24%, car affordability fell 11% ([Oliver Wyman, 2025](https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2025/may/how-oem-and-dealers-should-address-german-auto-affordability-crisis.html)).

The customers answered with their wallets, and the 2025 sales rankings are the verdict. Spain closed 2025 with 1,148,650 registrations, its strongest market in six years, and the best-selling car for the third year in a row was the Dacia Sandero, a budget model built in Romania. Second was the Renault Clio; third, remarkably, the Chinese-built MG ZS ([ANFAC data via Europa Press](https://www.europapress.es/motor/sector-00644/noticia-dacia-sandero-cierra-2025-coche-mas-vendido-espana-toyota-marca-mas-popular-20260102123138.html)). The pattern holds across the continent: the Sandero was Europe's best-selling car in 2025 for the second consecutive year, and it has been the best-seller to private buyers since 2017, while the entire European top ten is small, cheap, and mostly years old as a design ([ACEA](https://www.acea.auto/pc-registrations/new-car-registrations-1-8-in-2025-battery-electric-17-4-market-share/), [full-year rankings](https://bestsellingcarsblog.com/2026/01/europe-full-year-2025-bev-sales-up-29-7-to-19-5-share-dacia-sandero-and-renault-clio-dominate/)). When the continent's most sophisticated car industry finds its markets crowning its cheapest products, that is not a taste change. It is a purchasing-power signal.

In Spain, where I live, the consequence is visible on every street: the average passenger car is now 14.6 years old, almost 30% of the fleet is over twenty years old, and for every new car registered, more used cars over ten years old change hands ([ANFAC, 2026](https://anfac.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/NP-ANFAC-Ideauto.-Informe-parque-de-vehiculos-2025.pdf)). Here is the bitter irony for European policy: regulations designed to cut emissions made new cars so expensive that the fleet stopped renewing. A new Euro 6d car emits roughly 70% less NOx than a 14-year-old one, but only if somebody can afford to buy it. Cars pollute less; the fleet doesn't.

### The subscription detour

Facing shrinking volumes, several OEMs tried to recover the lost revenue through software subscriptions, and the episode is instructive. BMW shipped cars with seat heaters installed and charged a monthly fee to activate them; the backlash forced a retreat in 2023, with BMW's own sales chief conceding that *"people feel that they paid double, which was actually not true, but perception is reality"* ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/alistaircharlton/2023/09/07/bmw-drops-controversial-heated-seats-subscription-to-refocus-on-software-services/)). Mercedes offered a $1,200 yearly subscription to unlock acceleration its EV hardware could already deliver. I read these as symptoms of the same disease. Subscriptions for genuinely evolving software services can be legitimate; paywalling hardware the customer already bought, in a market where that customer is being priced out of new cars entirely, spends trust the industry cannot afford. Recurring revenue has to come from recurring value, and recurring value is precisely what a slow engineering organization cannot produce.

## Enter China, at a different clock speed

Into this exact gap, unaffordable European cars and aging fleets, stepped the Chinese OEMs. The strategy was decades in the making: since the first joint ventures in the 1980s, market access was traded for know-how, and European brands happily focused on selling premium cars to China's growing middle class. Somewhere along the way, the students finished learning. Chinese cars stopped being imitations and became competitive products, then became *faster-evolving* products, with the large screens, voice interfaces and digital features their customers actually wanted, backed by a local supply chain that dominates batteries and electronics.

The point is not that China is a different country with different costs. The point is that it built a different way of running an automotive industry. Its home market taught it: customers who bought more than half of their new cars electric in 2025, who expect a visible product improvement every year and over-the-air updates every month, and a domestic price war that eliminates anyone slow. Those conditions produced an operating model, and the model travels. The MG ZS on the Spanish podium and BYD growing 374% in Spain in 2025 are that model competing in Europe's home markets.

The numbers on speed are the ones that should terrify every European engineering executive. A Reuters investigation found Chinese automakers now develop an all-new or redesigned model in as little as 18 months, versus roughly five-year redesign cycles at legacy OEMs; the average Chinese-brand EV on sale is 1.6 years old, versus 5.4 years for foreign brands ([Reuters, 2025](https://www.reuters.com/investigations/how-chinas-new-auto-giants-left-gm-vw-tesla-dust-2025-07-03/)). AlixPartners quantifies the same gap: twice the development speed, with 40 to 50% less investment ([WardsAuto](https://www.wardsauto.com/news/automakers-catch-up-to-china-speed/822184/)). And this is not only a story about long hours. Chinese OEMs hand suppliers ~600 performance specs where a legacy OEM hands over 20,000 hyper-detailed ones; they run development phases in parallel instead of sequentially; they reuse proven components aggressively instead of redesigning them for every program.

The consumer trends born in that market are now reshaping what German and European manufacturers must build. Volkswagen's response is telling: it pays XPeng, a company founded in 2014, to co-develop the electronic architecture of its Chinese vehicles, extended that architecture from EVs to its combustion and hybrid platforms in 2025 ([XPENG and Volkswagen](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2025/08/15/3134027/0/en/XPENG-and-the-Volkswagen-Group-Announce-Entry-into-Agreement-on-Expanding-E-E-Architecture-Technical-Collaboration.html)), and set itself the target of being *"as fast and as competitive as a Chinese startup"*. At home, the December 2024 agreement with its unions cut more than 35,000 German jobs and 734,000 units of capacity ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/rapprochement-between-volkswagen-union-wage-talks-sources-say-2024-12-20/)). Farley, meanwhile, had a Xiaomi SU7 flown from Shanghai and drove it for six months ([The Drive](https://www.thedrive.com/news/jim-farley-drives-xiaomi-su7-ev)), calling China's EV progress "the most humbling thing I have ever seen" ([Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/ford-ceo-china-ev-progress-most-humbling-thing-ever-seen-2025-6)).

Legacy OEMs know it, and the honest ones are learning from it. Renault developed its new Twingo EV in 21 months at its Shanghai R&D center, against an estimated 42 months for its normal process, and saved roughly 40% of development cost doing so ([AP via Yahoo News](https://uk.news.yahoo.com/renaults-ev-reveals-global-auto-091200547.html)). Europe's response so far has been partly industrial and partly defensive: since October 2024 the EU applies countervailing duties of 17% (BYD) to 35.3% (SAIC) on top of the normal 10% tariff for Chinese-built EVs ([European Commission](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_24_5589/IP_24_5589_EN.pdf)). Tariffs buy time. They do not buy speed.

We have seen this movie before. In the 1980s, Japanese manufacturers arrived in the US with better quality, lower cost, and faster development cycles, and Detroit's first response was also to blame wages, currency and protectionism. What actually closed the gap was painful internal transformation: adopting lean production and rebuilding the development system itself. The competitor was never the problem; the clock speed was.

## Two tempting shortcuts, and why they disappoint

Under cost pressure, two escape routes look irresistible on a spreadsheet: move the engineering somewhere cheaper, or replace engineers with AI. I've watched both up close, and the research backs up what practitioners see.

**Offshoring.** Let me be precise about what the problem is *not*. It is not talent. India produces excellent engineers who reach the same quality standards as anyone, and you can hire a full team there for the salary of one senior engineer in Munich or Detroit. The problem is what you actually pay for, and it doesn't appear on the invoice. Decades of research on offshored software projects consistently find large hidden costs in requirements specification, knowledge transfer, control and coordination, costs that grow precisely when the work requires deep, client-specific system knowledge ([Dibbern et al., MIS Quarterly](https://doi.org/10.7892/boris.51792)), which describes essentially all vehicle control software. A detailed case study of a Swedish product company found that after accounting for overheads and productivity ramp-up, its offshored teams in India were still 50% *more* expensive per unit of output in year two than the mature home teams ([Šmite, Britto & van Solingen](https://rinivansolingen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/3_Calculating-the-extra-costs-and-the-bottom-line-hour-cost-of-offshoring.pdf)). Time zones, cultural distance and personnel turnover each add friction to every iteration loop. And that is the real loss: not money, not quality. *Iteration speed*, the one resource the market no longer forgives you for wasting. [The externalization article](/writing/externalization-trap-software-defined-world/) dissects this trap, and when outsourcing does and does not make sense, in detail.

**AI as head-count replacement.** The same logic error, newer clothes. The evidence for AI as a *substitute* for engineers is remarkably weak: in a randomized controlled trial, experienced developers using early-2025 AI tools on mature codebases were 19% *slower*, while believing they were 20% faster ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)). Corporate behavior is now catching up with the data: surveys find that over half of employers regret AI-motivated layoffs, and roughly two-thirds of companies that cut roles for AI have rehired a substantial share of them ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/01/employers-who-laid-off-workers-for-ai-are-reversing-their-decisions.html); [The Washington Times](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/mar/10/ai-layoff-reversal-companies-rehire-customer-roles-eliminated/)).

None of this makes AI useless; quite the opposite. Used as a *tool by engineers who understand the system*, it is a genuine accelerator: generating test scaffolding, exploring calibration spaces, virtualizing validation so problems surface before hardware exists, summarizing thousand-page requirement legacies. But in safety-critical embedded control, the bar is deterministic, explainable, certifiable behavior. AI can help us get there faster. It cannot yet be accountable for getting there. The judgment about whether a torque request is safe on a wet downhill with a full trailer still needs an engineer who has felt that situation. [The AI article in this series](/writing/ai-will-not-replace-automotive-engineers/) develops the full argument: what AI genuinely accelerates, and the four ways it damages organizations that adopt it carelessly.

## Buying speed: the new externalization

So what are the legacy groups actually doing? Something revealing: they're externalizing again, but this time they're not outsourcing *modules to the cheapest bidder*. They're buying *speed from software-native companies*. Volkswagen is investing up to $5.8 billion in a joint venture with Rivian to adopt its zonal architecture and software stack, a deal that was also a lifeline for the loss-making startup ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/rivian-volkswagen-group-launch-58-billion-joint-venture-2024-11-12/)). TRATON, the group that now spans Scania, MAN, International and Volkswagen Truck & Bus, announced a strategic partnership with Applied Intuition to build TRATON ONE OS, a common vehicle software platform and developer toolchain across all brands ([TRATON](https://traton.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/the-traton-group-and-applied-intuition-announce-strategic-partnership-on-vehicle-software.html)).

I read these deals two ways at once. They are an admission that the old organizations cannot generate the required pace internally; Cariad's balance sheet is the proof. But they are also a rational bet, *if* the OEMs use the partnership to rebuild their own capability rather than to replace it. TRATON's leadership said it plainly: the in-house competence stays, the partner complements it. The trap would be the Farley scenario in modern form: waking up in 2030 to discover that the defining layer of your product is, once again, someone else's IP. Integrating brands with decades of different engineering cultures (I lived through the early stages of Scania's integration into TRATON) is slow and expensive precisely when speed is the scarce resource; buying a common platform is a way to cut through that. The next few years will tell whether it worked.

There is also a quieter option between the spectacular platform deals and the classic low-cost offshore contract. If a company needs external capacity for work inside an active learning loop, the deciding variable is feedback latency, not hourly rate. Near-shore engineering teams, in the same or adjacent time zones, able to join the morning integration call, able to be physically at the test bench within a day, and working inside the OEM's own architecture and validation loop rather than behind a requirements wall, keep that latency low while adding capacity and cost flexibility. Research on outsourcing and temporal distance points the same way: for communication-intensive, iterative projects, nearshore development holds advantages that headline rates do not capture ([Looi and Szepan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08084)). The question for any sourcing decision is the one that runs through this whole series: does the arrangement shorten the learning loop or lengthen it?

## Near the product does not mean at the office

One more clarification belongs in this overview, because "keep engineers close to the machine" is routinely mistranslated into "everyone at their desk five days a week." Those are different things, and confusing them costs companies their best people.

Closeness to the product means driving the truck you programmed, standing at the HIL bench when your function misbehaves, walking to the test cell when the data looks wrong. It is a workflow built around the machine, not a badge swipe at 8:00. The evidence here is clearer than most opinions about it. A randomized controlled trial published in Nature, covering 1,612 university-graduate employees including software engineers, found that hybrid work with two home days per week had no measurable effect on performance reviews, promotions, or even lines of code, while cutting resignations by a third; the managers in the experiment expected productivity losses and revised their view after seeing the data ([Bloom, Han and Liang, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07500-2)). On the other side, studies of return-to-office mandates at S&P 500 firms found no improvement in firm performance or value ([Ding and Ma](https://www.business.pitt.edu/return-to-office-mandates-dont-improve-employee-or-company-performance/)), but a 14% rise in turnover concentrated among senior and highly skilled employees, and significantly longer times to fill the vacancies they created ([RTO and brain drain study](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2024/12/companies-issuing-rto-mandates-lose-their-best-talent-study/)).

For automotive engineering the honest conclusion is hybrid, designed around the machine. Test days, integration weeks, commissioning and calibration campaigns are physical and should be planned as physical. Analysis, implementation, simulation and documentation are not. An organization that trusts its engineers to be at the rig when the rig needs them, and at home when the work is a debugger and a log file, gets both proximity and retention. An organization that equates presence with commitment loses exactly the senior engineers whose system knowledge it cannot replace. Presence should be scheduled by the physics, not by the org chart.

## What I think a competitive organization looks like

Pulling the threads together, my conclusions after twelve years in this industry:

**Speed is the primary KPI now, and quality is downstream of it.** This sounds like heresy to anyone raised on the V-model, but fast iteration *is* a quality mechanism: more loops through real hardware per year means more defects found, more assumptions tested, more learning banked. The old processes guaranteed quality through documentation and gates; the new competitors achieve it through iteration count. Both work. Only one of them also ships a new platform in 18 months. [The learning-speed article](/writing/real-cost-of-automotive-software-speed/) defines the metric precisely: the time from observed physical symptom to validated, deployed improvement.

**Keep the critical software competence in-house, and keep it close to the machine.** The defining feature of the SDV era is that vehicle behavior is a software artifact. An organization that cannot modify its own product's behavior without a supplier's permission does not really own its product. Partnerships for platforms and tooling make sense; outsourcing the *understanding* never does. And the engineers writing control software should sit near, physically or organizationally, the mechanical engineers, the test rigs, and ideally the driver's seat. Every layer between the person who writes the function and the physics it controls is a tax on learning speed. [The externalization article](/writing/externalization-trap-software-defined-world/) turns this into a practical sourcing rule: selective ownership.

**Empower engineers, and make responsibilities explicit.** New E/E architectures, OTA pipelines and smart functions are never fully specified upfront; they converge through iteration. That demands genuinely cross-functional teams, agile ways of working extended beyond software islands into the whole organization, and, crucially in large groups, clarity about who decides what. In my experience the biggest failure mode of big-organization agility isn't slowness; it's important decisions being made badly, or not at all, because nobody had both the information and the mandate.

**Treat AI and offshoring as capacity tools, not capability replacements.** Both can extend a strong in-house team. Neither can substitute for one. The evidence says organizations that misread this spend two years discovering that the cheap option was the expensive one, and pay for it in the only currency that matters now. [The AI article](/writing/ai-will-not-replace-automotive-engineers/) spells out what strong adoption looks like: governed platforms, juniors trained with AI rather than replaced by it, and AI output measured by validated learning speed.

Embedded software, and embedded *control* in particular, is where all of this converges. Electrification, automation, emissions compliance, energy management, safety: every one of these is delivered through real-time software acting on physical systems. The industry likes to talk about apps and screens, but the layer that decides whether a truck is efficient, safe and legal is the control layer. That's true for vehicles, and increasingly true for every machine and energy system going through the same transformation. Companies that treat that layer as a core competence, and organize so it can learn fast, are the ones that will still be here in fifteen years.

The transformation is deep, the incumbents' disadvantages are real, and there is no short-term fix. But the direction is clear enough: the future belongs to organizations where the people who understand the physics can change the software, quickly.
