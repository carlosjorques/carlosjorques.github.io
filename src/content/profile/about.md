## Why Control Systems Matter

Long before control systems became software, engineers were already building feedback into real-world systems. A steam engine governor is one of my favorite examples: a mechanical device of rotating balls, levers, linkages, and valves that helped regulate speed. It is a classic control theory example because it shows how feedback made powerful machines more stable, usable, and productive.

What changed with embedded software was the scale of what control systems could become. Mechanisms that once required complex physical linkages could later be expressed as logic running in real time, connected to electronic sensors and actuators. As microprocessors, sensing, communication, and data storage evolved, control software made it possible to implement more advanced algorithms, calibrate behavior from real data, and coordinate increasingly complex functions in cyber-physical systems.

That is why control systems matter. They are often invisible, but they shape how real-world systems behave: vehicles manage energy, robots balance and move, buildings reduce consumption, batteries operate safely, factories coordinate demand, and power grids absorb more renewable energy. My own work sits inside that evolution, where feedback control, predictive control, diagnostics, calibration, and embedded software turn advanced ideas into reliable production behavior.

## From Robotics to Production Systems

As a child, I used to take toys apart just to see how they worked. I thought engineering meant inventing things, but what really fascinated me was understanding why things behaved the way they did.

### Taking things apart

That curiosity started early: opening toys, looking inside, and trying to understand the behavior behind the mechanism.

### Campus Party, Valencia

After building a basic obstacle-avoiding robot kit, I watched a line-tracking robot competition at Campus Party in Valencia. Some robots were fast and stable. Mine oscillated from side to side. I wanted to understand why.

### Control theory

At university, feedback control changed from an equation on a whiteboard into an explanation for real behavior: why a robot follows a line, a balancing system recovers, or a climate system stabilizes a room despite disturbances.

### Valencia to Lund

Industrial Engineering in Valencia led to a double degree in Electrical Engineering in Sweden, where I deepened my focus on automatic control, dynamic systems, and model-based engineering.

### Production systems

An industrial PhD and later production embedded control software connected theory with heavy vehicles, advanced functions, and the constraints of systems that need to work reliably in operation.

### Closing

What stayed constant through that path was the same question that started with toys and robots: how do you make a real-world system behave reliably when the world around it is changing?

## How I Approach Complex Systems

> An elegant algorithm is only the beginning. Production readiness is where engineering earns trust.

Complex engineering problems are rarely solved by one discipline in isolation. I try to help teams build a shared understanding of the system, the constraints, the risks, and the decisions needed to move from uncertainty to reliable product behavior.

### Build a shared system view

I help teams connect control objectives, software architecture, hardware constraints, data, safety requirements, calibration needs, and product expectations into one coherent picture.

### Understand the problem before choosing a solution

Root causes, control strategies, diagnostics, degradation logic, implementation details, and validation evidence are different layers. Keeping them clear helps teams avoid premature fixes and create solutions that are easier to test, explain, and maintain.

### Make trade-offs explicit

Technical decisions are made under constraints: time, resources, hardware limits, safety requirements, dependencies, and unexpected contingencies. I value making those trade-offs visible so teams can compare options, understand risk, and commit with clarity.

### Support teams from decision to delivery

Good technical leadership is not about controlling every detail. It is about giving engineers context, helping them anticipate problems, aligning interfaces and responsibilities, and supporting decisions until they become reliable production behavior.
